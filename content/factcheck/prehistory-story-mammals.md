## Included, left out, and why
- Triassic and Jurassic kept to one opening chapter (the dinosaurs and Great Dying stories already cover synapsids and the Karoo). It is anchored at 125 Ma in Liaoning because the Repenomamus fossils there are the most direct evidence that some Mesozoic mammals were not tiny. Morganucodon (205 Ma, Glamorgan) appears as a second site.
- Corral Bluffs and the K-Pg survivors are merged into one chapter to keep the count at 9; Purgatorius (Montana) is a second site.
- The Fayum primates (Aegyptopithecus) and the 2021–2023 Egyptian whale finds (Phiomicetus, Tutcetus) were left out to keep the whale chapter on one line of evidence; the existing event e:fayum-and-wadi-al-hitan covers the Fayum.
- Australia's marsupials (Riversleigh) and the Oligocene giants (Paraceratherium) were left out for length.
- Humans are only mentioned as one possible cause of the extinctions; the humans story is not repeated.

## Dates compared with existing project data
- Pakicetus: text says "about 49 million years ago", matching origins.json (whales, 49 Ma). Wikipedia gives c. 48.7–45.4 Ma (about 48.5). The site uses 48.5.
- PETM warming "about 5 to 8 °C in less than 10,000 years", copied from the event paleocene-eocene-thermal-maximum. Smith et al. 2006 call it a c. 100,000-year episode and EarthSky (Secord 2012) gives c. 175,000 years; the text gives no duration, so it does not conflict.
- Great American Interchange "about 2.7 million years ago", matching the event. Santa Fe River 1 is dated by the Florida Museum to c. 2.3–2.6 Ma (Blancan).
- Megafauna window "50,000–10,000 years ago", matching e:megafauna-extinctions.

## Disputed points (both sides)
- Causes of the megafauna extinction (chapter 9, evidence "disputed"): O'Keefe et al. 2023 (NHM Los Angeles press release) put the La Brea losses down to fire-driven ecosystem change, with warming, drying and growing human numbers; they reject overhunting by Clovis people and a comet. The existing event lists Sandom 2014 and Svenning 2024 (human pressure) against Cooper 2015 and Stewart 2021 (climate). The text says the cause is argued and probably varied by region.
- Wrangel mammoths: Dalén et al. 2024 (Cell) find inbreeding was not enough to doom them; the cause of the final extinction around 4,000 years ago is unknown.
- Placental origin: fossils alone point to an origin after 66 Ma; molecular clocks point to the Late Cretaceous. Carlisle et al. 2023 (Bristol) model the fossil record and find primates, rabbits and carnivorans originated just before the impact. The text only says Purgatorius appeared within about 140,000 years, which is not disputed.
- Purgatorius: sometimes seen as the earliest primate, sometimes as a basal plesiadapiform or outside crown placentals (Wikipedia, citing Chester 2015 and others). The text calls it "an early relative of the primates".
- Maiacetus birth on land: Gingerich et al. 2009 read the head-first fetus as evidence of birth on land; Thewissen suggested it may be a meal or displaced. The text says "may still have given birth on land" and the evidenceNote says this is disputed.
- Ambulocetus walking: a 2016 study doubted it could walk on land; the text only says it swam like an otter.
- Isthmus of Panama timing (chapter 7): O'Dea 2016 (c. 2.8 Ma) against Montes (middle Miocene closure), as already logged for e:isthmus-of-panama. Titanis in Texas at c. 5 Ma and ground sloths by c. 9 Ma show some animals crossed earlier, probably over water. Evidence is set to "fossil", because the exchange itself is not in doubt; the evidenceNote mentions the dispute.
- Darwinius: placed as a near-ancestor of monkeys by its describers, but as a lemur-line (strepsirrhine) primate by Seiffert and others. The text calls it only "the primate Darwinius".
- Messel deaths: volcanic gas, poisonous cyanobacteria and other causes have all been suggested; the text says so.
- Onychonycteris echolocation: lacked an enlarged cochlea, but a 2010 reanalysis suggested possible echolocation. The text says "may have flown before bats could hunt by echolocation".
- Ashfall: the museum names the Bruneau-Jarbidge caldera, south-west Idaho (nearly 900 miles away); the University of Cincinnati release says a "Yellowstone supervolcano" (the same hotspot track). The text says "a huge eruption in what is now south-western Idaho".
- Han et al. 2023 Repenomamus–Psittacosaurus fossil: Wikipedia calls the dinosaur an adult, while the study's press coverage says both animals were probably subadults. The text avoids giving the age.

## Source check, 2026-11-22

Every chapter's cited pages were fetched again and each claim in title, dateLabel, text, species, site notes and evidenceNote was checked against them; chapter "ma" values all fall inside their dateLabels and none was changed.

### Corrections
- [hothouse-newcomers] text: "warmed the world by about 5 to 8 °C in less than 10,000 years" → "in less than 20,000 years" — no cited page supported under 10,000 years; the PETM article gives the 5 to 8 °C rise and a rapid onset of the carbon isotope excursion of under 20,000 years (helium-3 records) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleocene%E2%80%93Eocene_Thermal_Maximum
- [eocene-lake-windows] text: "fox-sized early horses" → "small early horses" — no cited page gives a fox-sized figure for the Messel horses (the Messel article calls them pygmy horses; the Eurohippus article gives no size) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messel_pit
- [mammoth-steppe-giants] text: "its small ears and paws suggest an animal built for the cold" → "its small ears and wide paws suggest an animal built for the cold" — Lopatin et al. 2024 describe the paw as wide, with square pads and no carpal pad, as snow adaptations; only the ears are small — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11564651/
- [the-giants-disappear] text: "The last woolly rhinos lived in north-east Siberia about 14,000 years ago" → "The last reliably dated woolly rhinos lived in north-east Siberia about 14,000 years ago" — the cited article gives 14,000 years for the youngest reliable records but notes environmental DNA that may extend the species to about 9,800 years ago — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woolly_rhinoceros

### Sources added or replaced
- [small-mammals-dinosaur-world] Wikipedia: Yixian Formation replaced by Han et al. 2023, Scientific Reports (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-37545-8). The text's "a volcanic mudflow buried them both" was not on any cited page; the paper says a "lahar-type volcanic debris flow" and gives the Lujiatun beds' age as about 125.7 million years. The page now loads; the earlier log said it redirected to a login.
- [hothouse-newcomers] Wikipedia: Sifrhippus replaced by Wikipedia: Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum, for the size and speed of the warming. The Sifrhippus size figures are still covered by the EarthSky report on Secord et al. 2012.
- No cited source was dead. The Denver Museum page refuses scripted fetches (403) but loads in a browser-style fetch; it confirms Carsioptychus at 300,000 years, Eoconodon (wolf-sized, 680,000 years), Taeniolabis (34 to 56 kg) and "a 100-fold increase in body size compared to the tiny survivors". The EurekAlert Cell Press release also needed a browser-style fetch; it confirms Wrangel's isolation about 10,000 years ago and 6,000 years of survival there.

### Softened or not fully confirmed
- [whales-return-to-sea] "About 49 million years ago ... Pakicetus": the cited article gives about 48.5 million years (range 48.7 to 45.4). Left as is because it matches origins.json (whales, 49) and is within rounding of the oldest date; a later pass could make it "about 48 million".
- [eocene-lake-windows] site note "over 1,400 kinds of fossil plants and animals": the Messel article and Smith et al. 2024 (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12549-024-00633-2) give 1,409 taxa, including fungi. Taxa are not the same as species, since one plant can leave pollen, leaf and seed taxa. Kept, as "kinds" is loose enough.
- [mammals-rebound] "mammal diversity doubled within about 100,000 years" is in the University of Washington release, not on the Denver Museum page.
- [hothouse-newcomers] The Teilhardina "Asia to Europe to North America" route is hedged ("seems"); the Teilhardina article notes Wyoming finds once suggested a North American origin before the east-to-west model was preferred.
- [great-american-interchange] Titanis height: the Florida Museum gives about 1.5 m and Wikipedia 1.4 to 2 m. Kept at "about 1.5 metres". Wikipedia dates the Florida Titanis finds to 2.2 to 1.8 million years; the text keeps the Florida Museum's 2.6 to 2.3 million years for Santa Fe River 1.
- [the-giants-disappear] Wikipedia notes an environmental-DNA claim that mammoths survived on the Taymyr Peninsula until about 4,000 years ago, which has been questioned. The text's "mammoths died out on the mainland about 10,000 years ago" follows the mainstream radiocarbon view.
